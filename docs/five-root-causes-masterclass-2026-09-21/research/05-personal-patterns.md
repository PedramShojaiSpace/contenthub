# Personal Pattern Mapping and Better Questions

**Module:** Five Root Causes Masterclass (2026-09-21)  
**Use:** Wellness education only; not medical care, diagnosis, treatment, or a promise of causation.

## Purpose and learning outcome

This 8–10 minute module teaches participants to move from a **symptom story** (“I feel foggy and sleep badly”) to a **pattern map**: a time-bounded, neutral record of what was noticed, when it happened, what was happening around it, and what questions remain. The map is a conversation aid—not a self-diagnosis tool. The goal is to improve observation and clinician communication while preserving uncertainty.

A useful distinction is **signal, context, and interpretation**. Signal is the directly observed experience (for example, “woke three times” or “headache began after lunch”). Context is what co-occurred (schedule, caffeine, medication timing as prescribed, travel, room, stress, illness, menstrual cycle, or activity). Interpretation is the hypothesis (“this must be mold” or “my wearable says deep sleep is low”). Keep interpretation provisional until an appropriately qualified professional evaluates it.

## Practical teaching arc (8–10 minutes)

1. **0:00–1:00 — Set the frame.** Explain that a pattern map organizes observations; it does not identify a root cause. Invite participants to use a recent, non-emergency concern and to stop if tracking increases anxiety.
2. **1:00–2:15 — Symptom story versus map.** Demonstrate converting “I sleep terribly” into date/time, sleep opportunity, awakenings, daytime effect, and relevant context. Use neutral verbs: noticed, recorded, occurred alongside, improved while away—not caused by.
3. **2:15–4:00 — Build the five-row map.** Have learners record: (a) experience and intensity, (b) timing/duration/frequency, (c) setting or exposure context, (d) routines/medications/substances as relevant, and (e) function or safety impact. Include missing data and confidence (“memory,” “diary,” or “device estimate”).
4. **4:00–5:30 — Look for patterns without over-reading them.** Compare repeated episodes, weekdays versus weekends, locations, and before/after changes. Teach three cautions: co-occurrence is not causation; recall and recording can be selective; one person’s observations do not establish a general rule.
5. **5:30–7:00 — Consumer data reality check.** Treat wearable, app, home sensor, and questionnaire output as supplementary observations. Record device/model, date range, missingness, and whether the metric is measured or algorithmically inferred. Do not use a consumer sleep score to diagnose or rule out a disorder.
6. **7:00–8:45 — Turn the map into better questions.** Practice: “What could explain this pattern, and what else should we consider?” “Which details would change your evaluation?” “Would a diary, exam, laboratory test, or sleep study be appropriate for my situation?” “What should I track—and what should I stop tracking?”
7. **8:45–10:00 — Close with action and safety.** Bring a one- to two-week summary, not an overwhelming data dump; name the main concern, functional impact, relevant timeline, and questions. Seek timely professional help for concerning or worsening symptoms; use urgent services for emergencies.

## Five cautious factual claims with sources

1. **A sleep diary can help a healthcare professional understand sleep timing and activities that may affect sleep.** The NIH NHLBI suggests keeping a diary for 1–2 weeks and lists bedtime, wake time, naps, daytime sleepiness, caffeine/alcohol, and exercise as useful entries. [NHLBI, “Insomnia Diagnosis”](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/insomnia/diagnosis)
2. **Clinician evaluation generally uses symptoms and health history, and testing is situation-dependent.** NHLBI describes medical history and physical examination and notes that sleep studies, actigraphy, or blood tests may be used to look for particular problems; this does not mean every person needs testing. [NHLBI, “Insomnia Diagnosis”](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/insomnia/diagnosis)
3. **Regular sleep problems or signs of a sleep disorder are reasons to talk with a healthcare provider.** CDC lists difficulty falling asleep, repeated awakenings, and daytime sleepiness/tiredness as signs of poor sleep quality and advises discussing regular problems with a provider. [CDC, “About Sleep”](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about/index.html)
4. **Consumer sleep technologies have important validity and interpretation limits.** A peer-reviewed review notes inadequate or mixed evidence for validity/reliability across devices, proprietary algorithms, missing data, and firmware/device factors. [de Zambotti et al., 2019, *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6579636/)
5. **The AASM says consumer sleep technology should not be used to diagnose or treat sleep disorders, but may support a patient–clinician interaction when placed in an appropriate clinical evaluation.** [AASM Position Statement](https://aasm.org/advocacy/position-statements/consumer-sleep-technology/)

## Testing and clinician-conversation language

Use language that invites evaluation without promising an answer: “I am bringing a pattern summary, not a diagnosis.” “Could we review what other explanations should be considered?” “Which history, examination, or test—if any—would be appropriate, and what question would it address?” “What might this test miss or fail to explain?” “How should I interpret a normal, abnormal, or inconclusive result?” “Would you prefer a diary, selected device data, or no further tracking?” “What symptoms or changes should prompt earlier follow-up?”

Do not say: “This proves the cause,” “my app diagnosed me,” “a test will find the root cause,” or “normal results mean nothing is wrong.” A test is selected for a clinical question and interpreted with history and examination; it can inform decisions without resolving every symptom. Share medication and substance information accurately, without changing prescribed treatment based on a chart, app, or class exercise.

## Limits of consumer and self-collected data

Consumer data may be useful for noticing broad timing or behavior patterns, but it can be incomplete, noisy, non-comparable across devices, altered by software updates, and based on proprietary algorithms. A displayed sleep stage is not equivalent to a clinical sleep study. Home environmental sensors can show a reading at one location and time; they do not by themselves establish personal exposure, dose, or causation. Avoid repeated checking when it increases worry, and protect privacy by reviewing app permissions, cloud sharing, and who can access exports. Bring only the relevant date range and a short summary unless the clinician requests raw data.

## Safety and compliance notes

- This module is educational wellness content, not medical advice, diagnosis, treatment, prevention, or a substitute for a licensed clinician.
- Do not recommend stopping, starting, or changing medication, supplements, diet, devices, or environmental remediation based solely on a pattern map.
- Do not infer a universal “root cause” from temporal association, a single test, a wearable score, or a home sensor. Symptoms are often nonspecific and can have multiple contributors.
- Encourage appropriate clinician discussion for persistent, recurrent, worsening, or functionally significant symptoms. Sleepiness that could impair driving or operating machinery warrants immediate risk reduction; do not drive when drowsy.
- Encourage urgent/emergency care for severe breathing difficulty, chest pain, fainting, sudden neurologic changes, severe allergic reaction, poisoning concern, or other emergency symptoms; the class should not triage these conditions.
- Environmental concerns should be handled cautiously: avoid unsafe DIY inspection/remediation; use qualified local professionals and relevant public-health guidance when a hazard is suspected.
- Minimize data collection, de-identify worksheets where possible, and do not upload health or household data to the course unless the product has a clear, consented privacy process.
- Accessibility: offer a low-tech paper option, allow participants to opt out, and avoid implying that more tracking is always better.
